A Quick Financial Data Analysis to Sharpen My Analytical Skills

Sometimes the best way to stay sharp is to pick up a dataset you have never seen and make sense of it in an afternoon.

5 min read

My team lead handed me a financial statement dataset and said, "What can you find?" No spec. No requirements doc. Just raw data and an open question.

That is actually how most real analytical work starts. Someone gives you data and a vague question, and your job is to turn it into something useful. This project was my warmup, a quick drill to keep my Excel and data analysis fundamentals sharp.

Why I Built This

Fundamentals decay if you do not practice them. I spend most of my time in Python and Jupyter notebooks now, but Excel is still the lingua franca of business. When a VP asks you a question in a meeting, they do not want to wait for you to spin up a notebook. They want an answer in the next five minutes.

This project was deliberate practice. Take unfamiliar data, clean it, summarize it, find the story, and present it clearly. All within one sitting.

The Core Concepts, Explained Simply

Financial Statement Analysis. Financial data tells you how a business is doing. Revenue, expenses, margins, growth rates. The trick is knowing which numbers matter for which questions. Revenue growth looks great until you notice margins are shrinking, which means the company is growing but becoming less profitable.

Key Insight: The most valuable skill in data analysis is not the technique. It is the ability to look at a dataset you have never seen and quickly figure out what questions it can answer and what story it tells.

Pivot Tables. Pivot tables let you slice data across dimensions instantly. Revenue by region. Expenses by category. Growth by quarter. Think of them as a multi-dimensional lens you can rotate to see the data from any angle. In an interview, demonstrating fluency with pivot tables signals that you can explore data fast.

Data Cleaning. Real financial data has inconsistent formatting, missing values, duplicate entries, and columns that mean different things in different rows. Cleaning is not glamorous, but it is where trust in your analysis is built. If you skip it, every insight you produce is suspect.

What I Learned

  1. Speed matters in business contexts. Being able to go from raw data to a clear summary in an hour is a career-defining skill.
  2. Excel fluency is still valuable. Python is powerful, but Excel is where business decisions get made in real time.
  3. Ask "so what?" after every finding. A number is not an insight. An insight is a number plus its implication for a decision.
  4. Clean data first, always. Fifteen minutes of cleaning saves hours of debugging wrong conclusions.
