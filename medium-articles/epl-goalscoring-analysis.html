The Data Behind Premier League Goals: What Expected Goals Reveals

Goals win games. But understanding how, when, and where goals happen is what makes you a data scientist, not just a football fan.

8 min read

A player scores 15 goals in a season. Is that good? It depends. If their expected goals (xG) was 10, they are overperforming, either incredibly clinical or riding a streak of luck. If their xG was 20, they are actually underperforming despite the highlight reel.

That single insight, the gap between actual goals and expected goals, tells you more about a striker than their raw goal count ever could. This project digs into that gap and a dozen other metrics to build a complete picture of Premier League attacking performance.

This is not just a football project. It is a masterclass in multi-dimensional comparison analysis. The same techniques apply to evaluating sales reps, marketing channels, manufacturing lines, or any scenario where you compare performance across many metrics simultaneously.

Why I Built This

I wanted to practice visualization design under real constraints. When you have 15 metrics for 50 players, you cannot show everything at once. You have to make smart choices about what to show, how to show it, and what story to tell.

That is the same challenge every data analyst faces when building dashboards and reports for stakeholders who have five minutes of attention.

The Core Concepts, Explained Simply

Expected Goals (xG). Every shot in football gets assigned a probability of being a goal based on historical data: distance from goal, angle, body part, game situation. A penalty might be 0.76 xG. A 30-yard shot might be 0.03 xG. Sum up all the xG for a player's shots and you get their "expected" output. The gap between actual and expected is where the real story lives.

Key Insight: If 100 players shot from exactly the same position, xG tells you how many would score. Over a season, players who consistently beat their xG are either genuinely elite finishers or due for regression. Distinguishing between the two is the analyst's job.

Conversion Rate. Goals divided by shots. A 25% conversion rate is elite. A 5% rate means either bad luck or poor finishing. But context matters: if most shots come from distance, a low rate might be expected. This is why you never judge by a single metric.

Shot Accuracy. Shots on target divided by total shots. Combined with conversion rate, you get the full picture. High accuracy plus low conversion means the goalkeeper is saving everything. High accuracy plus high conversion means a clinical finisher.

Per-90 Normalization. A player who plays every minute has more opportunity to score than a substitute. Dividing by minutes played and multiplying by 90 gives you a fair "per full game" rate. The same concept applies in business: revenue per employee, cost per acquisition, tickets resolved per engineer.

Step 1: Collect Premier League attacker statistics: goals, shots, shots on target, xG, minutes played.

Step 2: Compute derived metrics. Conversion rate, xG overperformance, goals per 90, shot accuracy.

Step 3: Build comparison visualizations. Rank players by different metrics. The xG-vs-actual scatter plot is the centerpiece: the diagonal line represents performing exactly as expected, above means overperforming, below means underperforming.

Step 4: Assemble the narrative. Who is the best finisher? Who is getting lucky? Who is underrated? Who needs better service from their teammates?

What I Learned

  1. No single metric tells the whole story. Raw goals, goals per 90, xG, conversion rate, and qualitative context all matter together.
  2. Normalization makes comparisons fair. Per-90 stats are essential when players have different playing time.
  3. Visualization hierarchy matters. Start with the big picture, then drill into interesting questions. Do not dump 15 charts on the reader at once.
  4. The diagonal line is powerful. In any actual-vs-expected scatter plot, the diagonal instantly shows who overperforms and who underperforms.
  5. Context defeats metrics. A low conversion rate from a player who only shoots from outside the box is not the same as a low rate from a player who misses easy chances.

Why This Matters for Your Career

"How do you approach multi-dimensional comparison analysis?" Start with one key metric to rank, then add dimensions one at a time. Use scatter plots for two dimensions, radar charts for many. The goal is insight, not information overload.

"Explain a time you normalized data for fair comparison." Per-90 normalization is the perfect example. Same concept as revenue per employee or cost per acquisition. Different playing time means you cannot compare raw totals.

"What is xG and how would you explain it to a non-technical person?" "Imagine every shot in football history from a certain position. If 100 players shot from there, how many would score? That percentage is xG."

"How do you decide what to visualize vs. what to leave in a table?" Visualize patterns, relationships, and distributions. Use tables for precise numbers and reference lookups.
