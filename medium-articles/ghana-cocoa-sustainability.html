A Data Investigation Into Cocoa Sustainability in Ghana

The numbers tell a story that policy debates alone cannot. I went looking for it.

9 min read

Ghana is the world's second-largest cocoa producer. Cocoa is the backbone of its rural economy. And yet, most of the farmers who grow it earn less than two dollars a day.

That contradiction kept nagging at me. Global demand for chocolate keeps climbing, but yields per hectare are stagnating. Climate change is shifting growing zones northward. The pressure to expand farmland is eating into forests the planet desperately needs.

So I did what any data person would do. I stopped speculating and started measuring. I built a full exploratory data analysis that asks the hard questions about cocoa sustainability in Ghana, and I am sharing everything I found.

Why I Built This

This is not just an agricultural problem. It is an economics problem, a climate problem, and a human welfare problem rolled into one. The only way to untangle those threads is with data.

I gathered cocoa yield data, economic indicators, sustainability metrics, and climate variables. Then I conducted a domain-specific EDA that goes far beyond default histograms and scatter plots. Every analysis step was driven by a hypothesis, not a checklist.

Why this matters for your career: Domain-specific EDA on a real-world policy question is one of the most valuable skills you can demonstrate in an interview. It shows you can take a messy, multi-dimensional problem, define the right questions, find the data, clean it, analyze it, and communicate findings that could actually influence decisions.

Core Concepts

Agricultural data is uniquely challenging. It is seasonal, geographically variable, and subject to external shocks like pest outbreaks and price crashes. Analyzing it requires thinking about time series, spatial patterns, and confounding variables simultaneously.

Think of it like diagnosing why a student's grades vary. Is it the teacher? The subject? How much they slept? Cocoa yield analysis is the same kind of multi-factor investigation. Yield depends on rainfall, soil nutrients, tree age, farming practices, pest management, and economic incentives. Teasing apart these factors is the analytical challenge.

I defined sustainability with concrete, measurable indicators:

  • Yield per hectare: Are we producing more or less cocoa per unit of land over time?
  • Input costs vs. revenue: Can farmers afford fertilizer, pesticides, and labor at current prices?
  • Deforestation rate: How much forest is being cleared for new cocoa farmland?
  • Soil health: Are farming practices depleting or maintaining the soil?
  • Farmer income: Can farmers actually make a living?
Key insight: Moving from vague aspirations like "we want sustainable cocoa" to measurable indicators is a skill that transfers to any domain where sustainability is part of the conversation. Numbers make the invisible visible.

I also built simple but revealing economic models. Given current cocoa prices, input costs, and average yields, what is a farmer's net income per hectare? I varied the inputs to run scenarios: What if prices drop 20%? What if a subsidy reduces fertilizer costs by 30%? What if improved practices boost yield by 15%?

This is sensitivity analysis, and it is incredibly powerful for policy discussions. Instead of arguing in the abstract about whether a subsidy program is "worth it," I can show exactly how many farmers cross the poverty line under each scenario.

The pipeline starts with data gathering: national and regional yield, production volume, cultivated area, cocoa prices, input costs, farmer income surveys, deforestation rates, and climate data.

Cleaning is where the real work begins. Agricultural data is notoriously messy. I dealt with inconsistent reporting periods, unit conversions between metric tons and bags of cocoa, missing regional data, and discrepancies across sources.

From there, I map yield by region to identify high-performing and underperforming areas, overlay with rainfall and soil data to generate hypotheses, plot time series trends to spot inflection points, build cost-revenue models, and run scenario analyses. The final output is a set of evidence-based policy recommendations that a policymaker could actually act on.

What I Learned

  1. EDA is an art, not a checklist. Good exploratory analysis is driven by domain curiosity and hypothesis formation. Each analysis should answer a question or generate a new one.
  2. Context transforms numbers into meaning. A yield of 0.4 tons per hectare means nothing until you know the global average is 0.5 and the theoretical maximum is 2.0. Context is how you turn data into insight.
  3. Agricultural data is messy in unique ways. Seasonal patterns, geographic variation, inconsistent units, and data scarcity in rural regions create challenges that generic tutorials do not cover.
  4. Economic modeling does not require a PhD. Simple cost-revenue calculations and sensitivity analyses can generate powerful insights that inform real decisions. The math is basic; the insight is sophisticated.
  5. Data storytelling matters. The most rigorous analysis is useless if you cannot communicate it clearly. I learned to structure my notebooks as narratives: set up the question, walk through the evidence, deliver a clear conclusion.
  6. Sustainability is quantifiable. You can measure it. You can track it. And once you can measure it, you can improve it.

Why This Matters for Your Career

Domain-specific data analysis is one of the strongest signals you can send in a technical interview. Here are some questions this project prepares you to answer with confidence.

Q: Walk me through an exploratory data analysis you conducted.

Start with the business question: "What drives cocoa yield variation in Ghana?" Describe the data sources. Walk through the analytical steps: distribution analysis, regional comparison, time series trends, correlation between yield and potential drivers. Share a key finding, such as tree age and fertilizer access explaining more yield variation than climate variables. Emphasize how each step was driven by a hypothesis.

Q: How do you handle messy, real-world data?

I profile the data first: check types, null rates, value distributions, and cross-source consistency. For this project, I dealt with inconsistent units, missing regional data, and misaligned reporting periods. I document every cleaning decision and create validation checks so I know the cleaning did not introduce artifacts.

Q: What is the difference between correlation and causation in agricultural data?

Regions with higher rainfall might have higher yield, but is it the rainfall causing it, or is it that wetter regions also have better soil and more investment? I handle this by controlling for confounding variables, looking for natural experiments, and being explicit about the limitations of observational data.

Q: How do you communicate data findings to non-technical stakeholders?

Lead with the insight, not the method. "Farmers in the Western region earn 30% less per hectare than the national average, primarily due to aging cocoa trees" is actionable. "I ran a multivariate regression with an R-squared of 0.67" is not. Always include a "so what."
